01 — Overview
The Challenge

When watching together means juggling everything else

Movie lovers want to watch with their loved ones remotely — whether maintaining a long-distance relationship, bonding with friends, or staying connected with family. They value the shared reactions and real-time conversation, but are currently forced to juggle multiple apps for sync and voice chat, creating lag, disconnections, and a fractured experience.

Users need a better way to watch movies remotely — one that delivers all the camaraderie of being together, without the setup complexity that is currently ripping them out of the experience.

🎯

Goal

Design a native group viewing feature that drives habitual repeat use

👥

Target Users

Long-distance couples, friend groups, and families watching Netflix socially

📱

Platform

Mobile companion + Smart TV — designed around users' natural dual-screen behavior

02 — Research
User Research

Research first.
The problem defined itself.

Before any design decisions were made, I needed to understand how people actually experience remote watching — not what I expected to find, but what real users were doing, feeling, and struggling with.

I conducted 6 in-depth user interviews using contextual inquiry and directed storytelling, then synthesized themes through affinity mapping. Competitive and comparative analysis of Teleparty, Discord, Disney+ GroupWatch, Amazon Watch Party, and Apple SharePlay provided additional context. The research didn't confirm a direction — it created one.

What research surfaced

🔗

Everyone was using a patchwork of workarounds. Teleparty for sync, FaceTime for voice, texting for coordination. No single app solved the problem — users were the glue holding it together, and that exhaustion was eroding the habit.

⏱️

Sync breaks were emotional, not just technical. When playback fell out of time — especially after an unannounced pause — users didn't just lose sync. They lost the shared moment.

📱

Dual-screen behavior was already the norm. 5 of 6 participants had their phone in hand while watching TV. This wasn't a new behavior to design for; it was an existing one to design with.

🎙️

Voice was strongly preferred over video. 5 of 6 said no to video or "only if it's small and optional." Video felt like a performance — users wanted presence without the pressure.

😂

Reactions — not content — were what users missed most. It wasn't about watching the same show. It was about hearing someone laugh at the same moment.

✨

Setup friction was the make-or-break factor for habit. Users who had easy coordination watched together weekly. Users who found it cumbersome stopped entirely.

"One button on Netflix: 'Watch with ___'. It auto-syncs, opens a small video bubble of the other person, and has optional chat."
— Rachel Kim, 27, Marketing Coordinator, Detroit/Chicago
"Coordinating is annoying. If it's not easy, I just don't do it."
— Participant B, 33, used Teleparty during COVID, rarely now

What the research made clear

Across every interview, the same pattern emerged: the barrier wasn't the technology — it was the fragmentation. Users weren't struggling to watch together once set up. They were struggling to get set up at all. That insight became the foundation for everything that followed.

04 — Competitive
Competitive Analysis

The market told
a surprising story

Every major streaming service that launched a native group viewing feature eventually discontinued it — while a third-party browser extension with 20M+ users continued to thrive.

Critical Insight

Native group viewing features fail when they treat sync as the feature. The products that survive treat human connection as the feature — sync is just the enabler.

06 — Design
Wireframes & Prototyping

From concept to
clickable prototype

Solution Statement

By introducing a native watch party feature combining synchronized playback, integrated voice chat, and one-tap setup — enhanced by a mobile companion mode that lets users react on their phones while watching on TV — Netflix can transform remote viewing into a seamless, natural experience that strengthens retention and turns watching together into a sustainable habit.

The design went through three rounds: low-fidelity wireframes to validate flows, mid-fidelity to test layout decisions, and a high-fidelity Figma prototype for usability testing. The dual-screen model — TV for content, phone for interaction — emerged as the core interaction pattern.

Key Design Decision

Early testing showed users wanted reactions on the TV screen but chat on their phones. Splitting the interface across devices — rather than cramming everything onto one screen — reduced visual clutter and matched users' existing dual-screen behavior.

07 — Testing
Testing & Validation

Two rounds of testing,
real feedback, real changes

I ran two rounds of moderated usability testing on the Figma prototype. The goal: validate the core watch party flow and surface anything blocking the experience from feeling seamless and natural.

Testing Scenario

Rachel wants to watch Love Is Blind with her boyfriend Jake in Chicago. She finds the show on Netflix, starts a Watch Party, sends Jake the invite link, waits for him to connect, and transitions to Companion Mode on her phone while the show plays on TV.

What changed between Round 1 and Round 2

🧪 Round 1 — Problem

Invite step caused hesitation. User typed in the message box but couldn't tell if they were sending an invite or Jake was.

"I wasn't sure who was sending." — P1

✅ Round 2 — Fix & Result

Redesigned as a popup overlay with Netflix UI visible in background. Added an after-delay to auto-transition to the waiting room.

No participants hesitated on the invite step in Round 2.

🧪 Round 1 — Problem

Flow felt like 3 separate apps. Invite → waiting room → companion mode felt like navigating to entirely different products.

"Why did the page change? That doesn't feel right." — P1

✅ Round 2 — Fix & Result

Auto-transition via after-delay from waiting room → companion mode. Overlay treatment kept Netflix visible, making transitions feel like state changes.

Participants described the flow as "one experience."

🧪 Round 1 — Problem

"Jake pending" created anxiety. Users didn't know if Jake had seen the invite, was joining, or had declined.

"Is he already here?" — P1

✅ Round 2 — Fix & Result

Changed to "Jake connected ✓" with a checkmark. Status language now clearly communicates connection state.

Zero confusion about Jake's status in Round 2.

🧪 Round 1 — Problem

Companion mode had no orientation. Users landed on the companion screen with no clear sense of what to do.

⚠️ Round 2 — Still Iterating

TV view made more prominent; Mute/Leave controls de-emphasized. Cast button given glowing active state. Still needs stronger orientation cues.

Next step: contextual labels + first-launch tooltip.

Design changes driven by testing

→
Invite redesigned as a popup overlay — Netflix background peeks through so it doesn't feel like a separate screen. After-delay guides users into the waiting room.
→
"Jake pending" changed to "Jake connected ✓" — eliminating ambiguity about whether the invite was accepted.
→
Auto-transition added from waiting room to companion mode — removing the manual step that made the flow feel fragmented.
→
"Control from phone" prompt made more visually dominant — stronger visual weight than the "No, keep TV" dismiss option.
→
TV View made more prominent in companion mode — Mute and Leave controls de-emphasized so viewing context takes visual priority.
→
Status indicators updated — Rachel and Jake pills made less rounded and non-interactive to avoid looking like tappable buttons.
→
Cast button given a glowing treatment — visually signals active casting so users know content is live on the TV.

What improved between rounds

Invite hesitation: eliminated · Flow continuity: significantly improved · Jake status clarity: fully resolved · Companion mode orientation: improved, still iterating · Task completion: faster with fewer dead-end clicks

Metrics to track in Round 3: invite completion rate · time-to-party-start · companion mode adoption rate · task success rate

08 — Takeaways
Key Takeaways

What I learned

🏗️

Design for the ritual, not the feature

Users who watched together weekly weren't using a better app — they had an easier routine. The insight wasn't "better sync," it was "remove every reason not to start."

📉

Competitive failure is a design brief

Disney+ and Amazon's discontinued features weren't engineering failures — they were engagement failures. Understanding why they lost users shaped every decision made here.

📱

Meet users in their existing behavior

People were already using two screens. The companion app amplified what they were already doing naturally, rather than asking users to change their habits.

🎙️

Qualitative research changes designs

Without interviews, I would have built video chat. Research revealed voice was strongly preferred — that single finding reshaped the entire communication model.
