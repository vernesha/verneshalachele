01 — Overview
The Challenge

When watching together means juggling everything else

Movie lovers want to watch with their loved ones remotely — whether maintaining a long-distance relationship, bonding with friends, or staying connected with family. They value the shared reactions and real-time conversation, but are currently forced to juggle multiple apps for sync and voice chat, creating lag, disconnections, and a fractured experience.

Users need a better way to watch movies remotely — one that delivers all the camaraderie of being together, without the setup complexity that is currently ripping them out of the experience.

🎯

Goal

Design a native group viewing feature that drives habitual repeat use

👥

Target Users

Long-distance couples, friend groups, and families watching Netflix socially

📱

Platform

Mobile companion + Smart TV — designed around users' natural dual-screen behavior

02 — Research
User Research

Research first.
The problem defined itself.

Before any design decisions were made, I needed to understand how people actually experience remote watching — not what I expected to find, but what real users were doing, feeling, and struggling with.

I conducted 6 in-depth user interviews using contextual inquiry and directed storytelling, then synthesized themes through affinity mapping. Competitive and comparative analysis of Teleparty, Discord, Disney+ GroupWatch, Amazon Watch Party, and Apple SharePlay provided additional context. The research didn't confirm a direction — it created one.

What research surfaced

🔗

Everyone was using a patchwork of workarounds. Teleparty for sync, FaceTime for voice, texting for coordination. No single app solved the problem — users were the glue holding it together, and that exhaustion was eroding the habit.

⏱️

Sync breaks were emotional, not just technical. When playback fell out of time — especially after an unannounced pause — users didn't just lose sync. They lost the shared moment.

📱

Dual-screen behavior was already the norm. 5 of 6 participants had their phone in hand while watching TV. This wasn't a new behavior to design for; it was an existing one to design with.

🎙️

Voice was strongly preferred over video. 5 of 6 said no to video or "only if it's small and optional." Video felt like a performance — users wanted presence without the pressure.

😂

Reactions — not content — were what users missed most. It wasn't about watching the same show. It was about hearing someone laugh at the same moment.

✨

Setup friction was the make-or-break factor for habit. Users who had easy coordination watched together weekly. Users who found it cumbersome stopped entirely.

"One button on Netflix: 'Watch with ___'. It auto-syncs, opens a small video bubble of the other person, and has optional chat."
— Rachel Kim, 27, Marketing Coordinator, Detroit/Chicago
"Coordinating is annoying. If it's not easy, I just don't do it."
— Participant B, 33, used Teleparty during COVID, rarely now

What the research made clear

Across every interview, the same pattern emerged: the barrier wasn't the technology — it was the fragmentation. Users weren't struggling to watch together once set up. They were struggling to get set up at all. That insight became the foundation for everything that followed.

04 — Competitive
Competitive Analysis

The market told
a surprising story

Every major streaming service that launched a native group viewing feature eventually discontinued it — while a third-party browser extension with 20M+ users continued to thrive.

Critical Insight

Native group viewing features fail when they treat sync as the feature. The products that survive treat human connection as the feature — sync is just the enabler.

Low-Fidelity Wireframes

Lo-Fi v1 — Exploring Multiple Paths Across Devices

Wireframes weren't decoration — they were decisions. Before any hi-fi work began, I needed to validate the end-to-end flow: how does a user go from browsing to watching together in as few steps as possible? Each group below represents a problem I was actively solving, not just a screen I was drawing.

Flow 01 Start Party Flow
💡

Early sketches focused on reducing the CTA to a single tap — the feature had to feel native to Netflix's browse experience, not like a separate product. The challenge: where does "Watch Party" live without disrupting solo viewing?

Entry Point Home — Browse & Show Discovery
CTA Placement Show Detail — Watch Party alongside Play
Decision: The Watch Party CTA sits next to Play on the show detail page — the moment of intent. Not in a menu, not in settings. Surface-level placement removes the barrier of discovery entirely.
Flow 02 Invite Flow
💡

In the first round of low-fidelity wireframes, I explored the full cross-device system — including an additional "guest-side" flow that allowed Jake to join and manage the party from his own device. I also explored a QR code + iMessage prefill pattern to eliminate invite friction entirely.

Setup QR Code Popup — instant share options
Send iMessage Prefill — zero-friction invite
Lo-Fi v1 — Initial Exploration: Explored the full cross-device system, including an additional guest-side flow. This round surfaced which paths users naturally gravitated toward — and which caused confusion.
Flow 03 Waiting Room & Connection Flow
💡

The waiting room had to communicate real-time status clearly across two states — before and after Jake connects. I explored how to surface that status without creating anxiety or ambiguity about whether the invite had been seen.

State 1 Waiting — guest status, pre-connection
State 2 Connected — all guests ready to watch
Decision: Two explicit states — pending and connected — communicated through distinct visual treatments rather than a single ambiguous label.
Flow 04 Companion Mode Flow
💡

Companion Mode emerged as the core interaction pattern after research validated that 5 of 6 users already had their phones in hand while watching TV. Instead of designing a new behavior, I designed with an existing one. The phone becomes the control center — the TV stays clean.

Controls Mobile — voice, mute, leave, party status
Reactions Mobile — emoji reactions + live chat
Decision: Splitting the interface across devices reduced visual clutter on TV and matched users' natural dual-screen behavior. The companion app amplifies what users were already doing.
Flow 05 TV Playback Flow
💡

The TV screen is sacred. When Companion Mode is active, the TV shows clean playback with only a subtle party indicator — nothing competing with the content itself.

Playback TV — Companion Mode active, clean viewing
Decision: Every interaction lives on the phone. The show plays uninterrupted. That's the experience users described wanting — and the experience the system delivers.
Usability Testing — Round 1 Findings

What I learned — and what I changed

After the first round of lo-fi testing, six clear issues surfaced. Each one drove a specific mid-fi change. This is the evidence connecting exploration to execution.

🧪 Top Issues Identified
01
Invite flow confusion

Users weren't sure who was sending the link and thought the typing box was a chat field.

02
Disorienting transitions

Full-screen jumps felt like leaving Netflix entirely; users preferred staying in context.

03
Unclear waiting room states

"Jake pending" vs "Jake connected" wasn't visually distinct enough to parse quickly.

04
Weak Companion Mode hierarchy

TV view felt secondary; "Mute" and "Leave" were too visually dominant.

05
Overbuilt guest-side flow

Users didn't expect a separate "Jake's view" — it added friction and confusion.

06
Missing visual cues

Cast button needed emphasis; participant chips read as interactive when they weren't.

✓ What I Changed (Mid-Fi)
01
Simplified invite flow

Replaced full-screen transitions with a modal overlay — Netflix stays visible in background.

02
Clearer waiting room states

Added explicit "Pending → Connected ✓" states with a timed auto-transition.

03
Stronger Companion Mode hierarchy

Enlarged TV view, reduced chip size, demoted secondary controls visually.

04
Removed guest-side flow

Eliminated the entire "Jake's view" path — reduced to the core host experience.

05
Improved visual cues

Cast button given a glowing active state; chips made non-interactive in appearance.

06
Balanced control layout

Redistributed weight so primary Companion Mode actions are immediately obvious.

Scope Decision

The guest-side flow — a fully separate "Jake's view" for joining and managing the party — was removed after Round 1. Users didn't expect it, didn't look for it, and its presence created confusion about who controlled the session. Removing it sharpened the core flow and reduced onboarding friction for both host and guest.

Solution Statement

08 — Takeaways
Key Takeaways

What I learned

🏗️

Design for the ritual, not the feature

Users who watched together weekly weren't using a better app — they had an easier routine. The insight wasn't "better sync," it was "remove every reason not to start."

📉

Competitive failure is a design brief

Disney+ and Amazon's discontinued features weren't engineering failures — they were engagement failures. Understanding why they lost users shaped every decision made here.

📱

Meet users in their existing behavior

People were already using two screens. The companion app amplified what they were already doing naturally, rather than asking users to change their habits.

🎙️

Qualitative research changes designs

Without interviews, I would have built video chat. Research revealed voice was strongly preferred — that single finding reshaped the entire communication model.
